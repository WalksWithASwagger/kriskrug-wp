## AI Strategy and Geopolitical Competition

The conversation opens on the structural tension between sovereign, local AI and the gravitational pull of hyperscalers. Speaker 2 frames a “middle power” strategy as on‑prem, open‑model/open‑weights AI that avoids the cloud entirely—a deliberate rejection of merely buying tokens from non‑domestic hegemons. Speaker 1 underscores the practical barrier: on‑prem requires owning hardware, a high upfront cost that raises the entry bar for smaller entities. He cites a working federated alternative: the Netherlands’ “Surfera” supercompute cluster, a cross‑university consortium that distributes loads. The pair note such approaches might suit universities, provincial health services, and civil society institutions better than small companies.

They dig into industry dynamics. Speaker 1 stresses that this cycle’s innovation is unusually driven by product companies who are fiduciarily bound to push frontiers as far as possible. In that context, they review the Aleph Alpha sale to Cohere as a “middle power” gambit and discuss Cohere’s evolving ownership (British controlling interest, “Canadian/UK” posture to serve EU sovereignty needs while remaining outside the EU). Cohere’s “North” platform was purpose-built for RBC—governance, PII controls, high‑integrity execution, intervention mechanisms—and is now being commercialized beyond its bespoke origins. Yet Speaker 1 observes Cohere’s public client roster looks thin for its stage and spend, speculating that clients may keep options open or that major work remains undisclosed. Speaker 2 posits a darker room of dual‑use/sovereign defense contracts that only Cohere can fulfill to stringent criteria, hence the quiet.

Mistral is held up as a consistent exemplar of European sovereign open‑weights—transparent, firm in its approach, and already used in a significant commercial deployment for a French nonprofit (per Speaker 2). The pair close this segment crediting product‑driven innovation pressure, the national calculus around sovereignty, and the pragmatic challenges of on‑prem as the axis points for middle‑power AI.

## Canadian National Policy and Identity

The dialogue shifts to Canada’s stance vis‑à‑vis the US, centered on Mark Carney’s broader strategy—described by Speaker 2 as a $1.4 trillion investment posture in Toronto with a “divesting from the closest partnership” framing toward America. The flashpoint is opening airports to private investment. Speaker 1 argues YVR is a crown corporation with no profitability mandate and is therefore part of the public good, analogous to healthcare or Canada Post. Privatization, he warns, turns airports into extractive businesses—credit‑card trolleys, higher food prices, captive‑audience pricing—echoing BC Ferries becoming “shittier and more expensive.” He doubts travelers should shoulder long‑term costs for short‑term capital infusions.

An anecdote emerges: Speaker 2 recalls an assertion from YVR’s innovation lab head that roughly 55% of revenue came from licensing border control software/hardware, painting YVR as “a tech company,” though Speaker 1 is skeptical of such a high share. Still, both concede airports can be innovation testbeds with exportable tech and that multiple revenue streams exist.

They wrestle with identity formation. Speaker 1 resists defining Canadian values as the inverse of America’s; he prefers a positive, standalone values basis—public transit, healthcare, food access, earlier retirement. Speaker 2 laments a missed chance for a national referendum when US pressures rose, arguing a robust debate could have codified Canadian priorities and insulated policy discussions from reactive framing. He likens the uncritical consensus to a “patriot act” moment—when fear or antagonism accelerates overreach.

Travel anxiety surfaces: Speaker 1 admits avoiding flights to the US (e.g., Florida) to prevent a single TSA incident from derailing his life, highlighting how border uncertainty can chill mobility for professionals.

## Professional and Community Initiatives

Speaker 2’s nonprofit (BC AI) hits its one‑year mark with a 92% membership renewal, indicating strong member retention. He details a shift from “early believers” outreach to structured growth: more channels for memberships, better repeatability, and assets to enhance longevity (ultimately a building). Internet Archive Canada increased its commitment: from $5,000 for an Internet donors fellowship to $10,000, plus travel (airfare/hotel) for two presenters to attend Futureproof—doubling the program and subsidizing attendance. BC AI also joined Tectoria for $420, landing in its directory, with Futureproof listed on their site; Speaker 2 seeks mentorship from Tectoria’s long‑time ED, praising their self‑funded, asset‑building model and portfolio strategy.

On regional momentum, the Comox Valley subgroup is the fastest growing (≈10–12 members), buoyed by multiple organizers, municipal venues (Native Sons Hall, Florence Filberg Center), and community events (e.g., an aquaculture‑hosted summer barbecue). Speaker 2 is also bridging to Victoria (PROMs Victoria keynote after Futureproof; aligning with local groups), though attempts to partner with House of Owls for an AI social during Victoria Tech Week fell through due to internal politics among artist members.

Speaker 1 (EPAM) describes a period of elevated travel and workload after a portfolio lead departed for a tenure‑track role at the University of Chicago. He’s helping cover commitments (including Disney work) while hiring is pursued—preferably an East Coast placement to reduce overall travel time. He is pushing for EPAM to invest in Vancouver‑based applied research with UBC and possibly UVic (and is considering an agritech AI SIG connecting industry like Miraterra and coastal biotech with academic talent). He flags a recurring problem: many “collaboration” invites are in fact sponsorship pitches; he wants open‑source, value‑driving projects aligned with billable work. On pedagogy, he notes an existential crisis: with ubiquitous AI live coding, validating fundamentals without regressing to poor didactic formats (e.g., proctored, high‑pressure exams) has become difficult.

They discuss an Island‑area overdose prevention field trip organized by BC AI’s members to the Downtown Eastside; Speaker 1’s spouse is in that ecosystem and warns about siloed funding, lack of data sharing, and fractured care continuity. Speaker 2 expects the group to learn by encountering community boundaries and cautions that passion plus naiveté can inadvertently cross lines; he trusts certain leaders (e.g., Gordon) to center empathy and adapt.

## The Perils of Technology and a Post‑Truth World

They confront the friction of teaching CS fundamentals when AI tools make live coding ubiquitous. Speaker 1 articulates the fear that technology could outpace understanding to the point no one can repair core systems; reverting to high‑pressure, in‑room proctoring harms those who don’t test well and is poor pedagogy.

A harrowing deepfake anecdote: a friend’s elderly parents received a voice‑clone call mimicking their son (claiming he was jailed) and lost $13,000 via a check collected by a courier and cashed at a payday lender. This exemplifies the fragility of trust and the burden on families—especially older adults—navigating AI‑enabled fraud. Speaker 1 generalizes the crisis: in a “post‑truth” climate, many now judge veracity by outlet brand rather than content because assessing evidence is too hard.

The pair also explore AI companions for geriatrics. Speaker 1 describes designing avatars that communicate welcome and trust through micro‑behaviors—blinks, swallows, breathing, small fidgets—to draw engagement. While optimistic about near‑term utility (many teams are working on Alzheimer’s chatbots/companions), Speaker 1 worries about stratified dystopias where assistive tools double as ad channels (even deepfake “grandbaby” ads). They situate these anxieties amid current tensions: protests in Montreal, heavy security, and the vandalism of Mila—an institution doing some of the most aligned public‑interest AI work—suggesting that without dialogic space, outrage often finds the nearest target.
