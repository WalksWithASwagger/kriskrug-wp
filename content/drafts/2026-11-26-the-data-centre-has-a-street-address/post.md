## A month after BC voted two ways at once

Thursday I spent the day in the Asia Pacific Hall at SFU's [Morris J. Wosk Centre for Dialogue](https://sfu.ca/dialogue/about/history.html), 580 West Hastings, for the second Bridging the Political Divide dialogue.

The building went up in 1920 as a Toronto Dominion Bank branch. SFU calls it the only known facility in North America purpose-built for convening dialogue. A room built to hold money, rebuilt to hold arguments. I'll take it.

The timing was the whole point. On October 17, Vancouver elected [Pete Fry as the city's first Green mayor](https://vancouver.ca/news-calendar/official-2026-vancouver-election-results-oct-2026.aspx), with a progressive council beside him. One week later the BC Conservatives took [64 of 93 seats](https://thehub.ca/2026/10/26/b-c-conservatives-win-majority-as-ebys-snap-election-gamble-ends-ndp-rule/) in Eby's snap election, and Eby resigned as NDP leader.

Same province. Same month. The city went one way, the province went the other, and every person in that room walked in carrying their own theory of why.

## What this thing is

Bridging the Political Divide is one of two parts of [When the Map Is Useless](https://www.sfu.ca/dialogue/what-we-do/initiatives/map-is-useless.html), a multi-year SFU Centre for Dialogue initiative about how we make sense of things together once the old maps stop matching the ground.

The move is easy to say and hard to pull off. Put people with real influence over public life at the same tables: elected and former officials, journalists, union leaders, philanthropists, academics, community organizers. Then show that you can disagree hard without treating each other like garbage.

The first dialogue, in April, spent a day on what we even mean by democracy. This one got its hands dirty. Root causes of the divide. A debrief of both elections. And a deliberate push to pull in more of the spectrum than last time.

It runs under the [Chatham House Rule](https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule). You can use what you heard. You can't say who said it, or who was in the room. So everything here is either my own words or ideas that floated around the tables with the names stripped off. If you were there and want a shout, tell me and I'll add you.

## I never got angry. I got snide.

The day opened with an exercise. Pick the moment in a recent election that made you angriest, or happiest. Then dig for the value buried under it.

The theory goes like this. The values we write down are the ones we want people to see. The values that make our blood move are the ones actually driving. When you're furious, odds are one of those got stepped on.

My honest answer caught me off guard. I checked in with myself and realized I never really got angry this fall. I got snide.

I spent a lot of the last few months at the data centre demos, helping host conversations, trying to build a container where people don't have to scream at each other through megaphones. And there was one guy who was always the loudest voice there. Always the most absolute position on offer. Megaphone in one hand, his own photographer at his shoulder. By the third or fourth demo I could finish his sentences for him. I caught myself cracking jokes to friends about "our problem child."

It felt like he was stoking the fire so he could ride it into office. Maybe that's unfair. I get why a young newcomer would grab that energy and aim it at a ballot box. But that's what it felt like from where I stood.

So what was the value under the snark? Trust. Integrity. Respect for the slow, unglamorous work of building coalitions that don't eat themselves.

Not my proudest emotion. Turned out to be a pretty good compass though.

>>> The values we write down are the ones we want people to see. The values that make our blood move are the ones actually driving.

## The data centre has a street address

The thing that cooled me down traces back to Futureproof. On the last morning of the [festival](https://futureproof.website/), Naomi Klein sat down for a conversation about *End Times Fascism*, the book she wrote with Astra Taylor, and about where to push back. I've been chewing on what I took from it almost every day since.

Here's my version. Most of what scares people about technology has nowhere to protest. No office. No letter to write. No sidewalk to stand on with a sign. A data centre is different. It has a street address and a hydro hookup. You can stand in front of it. You can call your MLA about it.

So people show up carrying everything at once. Loneliness. Rent. Big tech. Microsoft. The climate. And if you answer all of that with water usage stats, you've already stopped listening.

The awkward part: a lot of the time, the local facts are on the builders' side. Plenty of protesters are fighting the American hyperscaler version, which isn't what's actually being proposed here. Doesn't matter. You can win every argument about megawatts and still miss what they're telling you, which is this:

*I feel a lack of agency in my relationship to technology and power.*

>>> You can win every argument about megawatts and still miss what they're telling you.

Hear that and the whole conversation changes. Now you can sit down and talk about the stuff that matters instead of trading stats like baseball cards. (I took a run at what real public benefit from local data centres could look like in [what I asked council last week](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/18/vancouver-ai-data-centres-public-benefit/).)

## Both hands full

This is [the stance I keep coming back to](https://kriskrug.co/2026/01/24/both-hands-full/) with BC + AI. One hand curious and building. The other hand critical, demanding transparency, wanting to know exactly what's getting plugged into our grid. At minimum, peel back fifteen years of obfuscation so we know what we're signing up for.

Some of my own crowd asks why I'd push to regulate the very thing we build with. That's exactly why. If we want people to trust what we make, we have to be the ones asking the hard questions first. Out loud. On the record.

It's changed how I show up at protests, too. I can stand beside someone whose sign I only half agree with and still hear the half that's true.

## What else came up at the tables

No names, per the rule. These are the threads I'm still pulling on.

- **Values on paper vs values in decisions.** Every org has a values page in its strategic plan. Almost none let those values steer an actual decision. One idea floating around: every new government names the values that will guide it, then shows its work in public.
- **Gendered attacks are a strategy.** When a candidate's worst childhood trauma becomes campaign material and a whole party lines up behind it, somebody decided that. On purpose.
- **Young people show up when politics shows up for them.** Housing, affordability, jobs, health care. Give them belonging and real roles, more than a door-knocking shift. And one election's youth spike is a moment. Don't call it a trend until it happens twice.
- **Organizing should be fun.** Food, music, art, kids welcome. People join for the cause. They stay for the belonging, and for feeling like their piece counts.
- **Facts alone don't move people.** On AI infrastructure and Canadian sovereignty, stories about local benefit matter as much as the numbers. And small founders can't just pack up for the US the way big companies threaten to. Family, immigration and roots keep people here.
- **Childcare is democracy infrastructure.** People shared stories of paying $200 a day. A program built for everyone tends to survive elections. One built for a narrow few gets cut.

## The homework

The organizers sent us home with an assignment, the [Bridging the Political Divide Home Challenge](https://www.sfu.ca/dialogue/what-we-do/initiatives/map-is-useless.html). Host up to six people at your place, including at least one person you disagree with. Then report back on how it went and what got in the way.

They're also working on a podcast, so these ideas can travel beyond the room without breaking the rule. The next dialogue is set for March 22.

I'm in. If you think I'm dead wrong about AI, data centres or anything else, you're exactly who I want at my table. Get in touch.

Who's coming to dinner?
