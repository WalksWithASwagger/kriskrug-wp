## A month after BC voted two ways at once

Thursday I spent the day in the Asia Pacific Hall at SFU's [Morris J. Wosk Centre for Dialogue](https://sfu.ca/dialogue/about/history.html), 580 West Hastings, for the second Bridging the Political Divide dialogue.

The building went up in 1920 as a Toronto Dominion Bank branch. SFU calls it the only known facility in North America purpose-built for convening dialogue. A room built to hold money, rebuilt to hold arguments. I'll take it.

I've been in that room before. Two Decembers ago I showed the AutoLoom portrait project at SFU's [Dialogue on Technology](https://kriskrug.beehiiv.com/p/vancouver-ai-revolution) summit, same building. That project has since put out some of the most useful numbers we have on how British Columbians actually feel about AI. More on those below.

The timing this time was the whole point. On October 17, Vancouver elected [Pete Fry as the city's first Green mayor](https://vancouver.ca/news-calendar/official-2026-vancouver-election-results-oct-2026.aspx), with a progressive council beside him. One week later the BC Conservatives took [64 of 93 seats](https://thehub.ca/2026/10/26/b-c-conservatives-win-majority-as-ebys-snap-election-gamble-ends-ndp-rule/) in Eby's snap election, and Eby resigned as NDP leader.

Same province. Same month. The city went one way, the province went the other, and every person in that room walked in carrying their own theory of why.

Here's mine, for the record. I've lived in Canada since 1995. I couldn't vote in either of those elections. I started my citizenship application this month, partly so that next time I can do more than write about it.

## What this thing is

Bridging the Political Divide is one of two parts of [When the Map Is Useless](https://www.sfu.ca/dialogue/what-we-do/initiatives/map-is-useless.html), a multi-year SFU Centre for Dialogue initiative about how we make sense of things together once the old maps stop matching the ground.

The move is easy to say and hard to pull off. Put people with real influence over public life at the same tables: elected and former officials, journalists, union leaders, philanthropists, academics, community organizers. Then show that you can disagree hard without treating each other like garbage.

The first dialogue, in April, spent a day on what we even mean by democracy. This one got its hands dirty. Root causes of the divide. A debrief of both elections. And a deliberate push to pull in more of the spectrum than last time.

It runs under the [Chatham House Rule](https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule). You can use what you heard. You can't say who said it, or who was in the room. So nobody gets quoted here. What you get is my own words, the ideas that moved around the tables with the names stripped off, and where those ideas collide with what I've been doing and writing about all year.

## I never got angry. I got snide.

The day opened with an exercise. Pick the moment in a recent election that made you angriest, or happiest. Then dig for the value buried under it.

The theory goes like this. The values we write down are the ones we want people to see. The values that make our blood move are the ones actually driving. When you're furious, odds are one of those got stepped on.

My honest answer caught me off guard. I checked in with myself and realized I never really got angry this fall. I got snide.

I spent a lot of the last few months at the data centre demos, [photographing](https://kriskrug.co/2026/10/04/whos-the-thirsty-little-democracy/), helping host conversations, trying to build a container where people don't have to scream at each other through megaphones. And there was one guy who was always the loudest voice there. Always the most absolute position on offer. Megaphone in one hand, his own photographer at his shoulder. By the third or fourth demo I could finish his sentences for him. I caught myself cracking jokes to friends about "our problem child."

It felt like he was stoking the fire so he could ride it into office. Maybe that's unfair. I get why a young newcomer would grab that energy and aim it at a ballot box. But that's what it felt like from where I stood.

![A red marker adds lettering to a cardboard sign laid out on the grass.](media:12909)
*Making signs before the October 3 data centre protest, Vancouver. Photo: Kris Krüg.*

So what was the value under the snark? Trust. Integrity. Respect for the slow, unglamorous work of building coalitions that don't eat themselves.

Here's the part that stung. When students [brought pots and pans to the AMS Nest](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/02/you-cant-rent-a-social-licence/) to protest an AI summit this month, I wrote that I knew the noise was inconvenient and I also wanted to know what made people bring the pots. I gave a whole building full of strangers that grace. I didn't give it to the one guy I saw every week.

Snark is what contempt looks like when it's trying to stay polite. And contempt is the thing this whole dialogue exists to take apart. Not my proudest discovery. Turned out to be a pretty good compass though.

>>> Snark is what contempt looks like when it's trying to stay polite.

## The data centre has a street address

The thing that cooled me down traces back to Futureproof. On the last morning of the [festival](https://futureproof.website/), Naomi Klein sat down for a conversation about *End Times Fascism*, the book she wrote with Astra Taylor, and about where to push back. I've been chewing on what I took from it almost every day since.

Here's my version. Most of what scares people about technology has nowhere to protest. The model lives in somebody else's cloud. The terms of service live in somebody else's legal department. The algorithm that decided your feed, your rent or your job application has no office, no letter to write, no sidewalk to stand on with a sign.

A data centre is different. It has a street address and a hydro hookup. It needs a permit, a rezoning, a grid connection. You can stand in front of it. You can call your MLA about it. You can register to speak at council about it, which is exactly what [I did on November 18](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/18/vancouver-ai-data-centres-public-benefit/).

So people show up carrying everything at once. Loneliness. Rent. Big tech. Microsoft. The climate. The job that got automated. The art that got scraped. When I walked with [Vancouver's first big anti-AI march](https://kriskrug.co/2026/05/23/you-cant-drink-data/) in May, the signs were about water, jobs, creative work and the right to think for yourself. One building ends up carrying all of it.

![A handmade sign reads People need WATER not AI.](media:11978)
*Vancouver, May 23, 2026. A demand about water, carried into the street. Photo: Kris Krüg.*

And if you answer all of that with water usage stats, you've already stopped listening.

The awkward part: a lot of the time, the local facts are on the builders' side. Plenty of protesters are fighting the American hyperscaler version, which isn't what's actually being proposed here. Doesn't matter. You can win every argument about megawatts and still miss what they're telling you, which is this:

*I feel a lack of agency in my relationship to technology and power.*

>>> You can win every argument about megawatts and still miss what they're telling you.

Hear that and the whole conversation changes. Stop trading stats like baseball cards and start asking the question underneath: who gets a say?

## The numbers say people aren't confused. They're unconvinced.

The lazy read on all this is that people just don't understand the technology yet. Teach them more and the fear goes away. That read is wrong, and the evidence came out of the same SFU centre I was sitting in.

The Dialogue on Technology [survey this summer](https://www.sfu.ca/sfunews/stories/2026/07/british-columbians-becoming-more-wary-of-ai-as-awareness-grows--.html) found awareness and use of AI rising in BC, and concern rising right along with it. Seventy-nine per cent were more concerned than excited. More than three quarters didn't trust tech companies to develop it with the public's wellbeing in mind. I wrote it up for BC + AI as [The AI Trust Gap Is Not an Education Problem](https://bc-ai.ca/news/adoption-is-not-the-goal).

National numbers rhyme. Angus Reid found [68 per cent of Canadians](https://angusreid.org/ai-regulation-canada/) want heavy regulation of AI companies even if it slows things down, and 74 per cent doubt any government can keep up. Protect us from something we doubt you can control. That's the mandate.

And then the one I find most hopeful. Léger found [81 per cent worried](https://leger360.com/ai-data-centres-canada/) data centres could push up their power bills, and 46 per cent calling their development a good thing anyway. People can see a benefit and still demand limits. Call it what it is: a negotiating position.

If a workshop only counts as a success when somebody adopts the product, we've quietly made the vendor's goal the student's goal. I teach this stuff for a living. Sometimes a good education ends with somebody recognizing a deployment they want stopped. That's the education working.

## You can't rent a social licence

This is where the dialogue and the data centre fight become the same conversation.

A social licence is the public trust that lets something take root in a community. You can't buy it. You can't PR it. And here's what I keep coming back to: [trust has to stay a judgment people can withhold](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/02/you-cant-rent-a-social-licence/). If the invitation to participate already has the answer filled in, it's an announcement with chairs.

![People gather around the atrium stairs inside the AMS Nest, with a UBC DIVEST banner stretched across the rail.](media:12959)
*The November 2 protest inside UBC's AMS Nest. Photo: Kris Krüg.*

So the question for every consultation, every summit, every dialogue, including the one I spent Thursday in: are we inviting people to shape the future, or to sit through its announcement?

We've tried to do the slower version ourselves. In September our [AI Ethical Futures Lab](https://bc-ai.ca/communities/futures-lab/ai-transparency) worked through Ottawa's AI transparency questions with about twenty people in Vancouver. Peter Van Garderen facilitated, Tanya Slingsby hosted at Parker Street Studios, and Sara Taherzadeh wrote it up. The doubt in that room was the same doubt I heard Thursday: will any of this change anything? So the submission asks the federal government to say which recommendations it accepts, defers or rejects, and why. Show your work. It's the same ask every table at the Wosk Centre kept circling.

![AI Ethical Futures Lab participants gathered for a group photograph, with the Lab name above them.](media:12770)
*AI Ethical Futures Lab, session seven, Mount Pleasant Community Centre, September 1, 2026. Photo: Kris Krüg.*

The best bridging I saw this fall happened on a lawn. At the [October 3 protest](https://kriskrug.co/2026/10/04/whos-the-thirsty-little-democracy/), the organizers openly admitted they disagreed. Some wanted enforceable rules. Some wanted to stop the projects outright. A moratorium was the floor they could all stand on together. An imperfect coalition that could still agree on the next decision it wanted changed.

![A group talks on the lawn with leaflets in hand, while someone writes on a sign behind them.](media:12911)
*The October 3 protest, Vancouver. Photo: Kris Krüg.*

That same afternoon, Cedar George-Parker opened with the moss and his grandmother's definition of rich: how much you give. Then a council candidate got into public power, public land, data residency and who controls the systems. Those are questions that survive whether you think AI is miraculous, monstrous or mostly a very confident autocomplete with a marketing budget.

That's my working test for a bridge now. Does the question survive both sides of the argument? If it does, you can build on it.

## What a bridge looks like at city hall

On November 18 I went to council to speak to [Motion 10, Addressing the Risks of Digital Infrastructure](https://council.vancouver.ca/20261118/documents/cfscmotion10.pdf). It passed unanimously. It asks city staff to come back with independent impact assessments, noise protections, enforceable commitments and annual public disclosure, and to build a framework before any more approvals.

I put two questions to council, and I'll put them to you:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Br6YTgcdZGY
*City of Vancouver's recording of the November 18 Standing Committee on City Finance and Services. My remarks are on Motion 10.*

>>> Is the harm acceptable, and does the public share in the returns?

People who love data centres and people who hate them can both work with those two questions. That's the point.

For the second one, I pitched a library card for compute. Public access to GPU time for students, researchers, nonprofits, startups and Indigenous-led projects, with actual humans there to help you use it. The [Compute Community Centre proposal](https://economyofwisdom.org/compute-community-centre/) from the Economy of Wisdom Foundation puts a price tag on that idea worth arguing about. Walk in with a question before you have a company, a cloud budget or a pitch deck.

![Economy of Wisdom's Compute Community Centre diagram: 12 homes, 48 public terminals and 19 GPU nodes.](media:13030)
*The Compute Community Centre, from Sev Geraskin's October 29 presentation at Futureproof. Original diagram: Sev Geraskin / Economy of Wisdom. Figures are proposal estimates; no site or funding has been secured.*

That idea clicked even harder for me at the dialogue, and I'll get to why.

## Both hands full

This is [the stance I keep coming back to](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/24/both-things-are-true/) with BC + AI. One hand curious and building. The other hand critical, demanding transparency, wanting to know exactly what's getting plugged into our grid. At minimum, peel back fifteen years of obfuscation so we know what we're signing up for.

People hear "both hands full" and assume I'm splitting the difference. I'm not. Fence-sitting is refusing to take a position. Both hands full is taking two at once. I followed the same logic out to the grid in [Both Hands on the Power Cord](https://kriskrug.co/2026/08/03/both-hands-on-the-power-cord/).

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-c7mgY2aSgM&t=245s
*Both Hands Full at LaSalle College Vancouver, Q&A included. The line the whole talk hangs on lands at 4:05.*

Some of my own crowd asks why I'd push to regulate the very thing we build with. That's exactly why. If we want people to trust what we make, we have to be the ones asking the hard questions first. Out loud. On the record.

And if all the thoughtful, critical people walk out, the future gets made by the hype people and the tech bros. I said that [in a parking lot to Bob Mackin](https://kriskrug.co/2026/09/07/ai-already-campaign-issue-vancouver/) back in July, months before the votes. Stay in the room. The room is making decisions whether you're in it or not.

## What came up at the tables, and where it lands for me

No names, per the rule. These are the threads I'm still pulling on, with my own receipts attached.

**Values on paper vs values in decisions.** Every org has a values page in its strategic plan. Almost none let those values steer an actual decision. One idea floating around: every new government names the values that will guide it, then shows its work in public.

Two days before the dialogue, BC published 24 mandate letters, more than 200 assignments. I read all of them. AI shows up in [exactly one](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/25/bc-mandate-letters-ai-strategy/), with no date and no safeguards. The province had a Minister of State for AI for sixteen months, and the new cabinet [quietly dropped it](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/24/bc-deleted-its-ai-minister/). That's a values statement too. Just not one anybody wrote down.

**Gendered attacks are a strategy.** When a candidate's worst childhood trauma becomes campaign material and a whole party lines up behind it, somebody decided that. On purpose. Name it as a choice, because that's what it was.

**Young people show up when politics shows up for them.** Housing, affordability, jobs, health care. Give them belonging and real roles, more than a door-knocking shift. And one election's youth spike is a moment. Don't call it a trend until it happens twice.

What stuck with me at the May march was how young that crowd was. People being promised an AI future were already arguing over the terms. If you're twenty and not pissed off right now, you're not paying attention. The job is to give that anger somewhere to land besides a megaphone.

**Access is a values question.** Two people from Surrey once came to one of our Vancouver gatherings and told me [what it cost them to get there](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/09/who-gets-a-say-canada-ai-future-canada-now/), in money and in hours on transit. Every "broader table" has a bus fare, a ticket price and a time slot that decide who actually sits at it.

**Organizing should be fun.** Food, music, art, kids welcome. People join for the cause. They stay for the belonging, and for feeling like their piece counts. This is the whole reason our meetups [don't do panels, they do portals](https://kriskrug.co/2025/04/07/we-dont-do-panels-we-do-portals/). Nobody builds a movement out of a slide deck.

![Festival attendees sharing lunch and conversation beside the windows.](media:12938)
*Lunch between sessions at Futureproof, October 29, 2026. Photo: Michelle Diamond / Diamond's Edge Photography.*

**Facts alone don't move people.** On AI infrastructure and Canadian sovereignty, stories about local benefit matter as much as the numbers. Small founders can't just pack up for the US the way big companies threaten to. Family, immigration and roots keep people here. Which is also why "sovereign" has to mean more than [servers with a Canadian address](https://kriskrug.co/2026/06/16/sovereign-ai-for-whom/). Sovereign for whom?

**Universal programs survive elections.** People shared stories of paying $200 a day for childcare. A program built for everyone tends to survive a change in government. One built for a narrow few gets cut.

This is the one that rewired my thinking about compute. A tax credit for AI companies is a narrow-few program. The next government kills it, or the next downturn does. A public library card for compute, open to the student, the nonprofit and the founder alike, is the childcare model. Build the public benefit for everybody and it has a fighting chance of outliving whoever's in charge.

>>> Build the public benefit for everybody and it has a fighting chance of outliving whoever's in charge.

## The homework

The organizers sent us home with an assignment, the [Bridging the Political Divide Home Challenge](https://www.sfu.ca/dialogue/what-we-do/initiatives/map-is-useless.html). Host up to six people at your place, including at least one person you disagree with. Then report back on how it went and what got in the way.

They're also working on a podcast, so these ideas can travel beyond the room without breaking the rule. The next dialogue is set for March 22, 2027.

I'm adding my own homework to theirs. In the Both Hands Full talk there's a fifteen-minute exercise: write your critique, write your curiosity, then write the one statement you'd defend holding both in a room of strangers. Bring that to dinner. Make everybody at the table write one.

Mine, for the record: the data centre protests are right about power and agency, and wrong if they think the answer is no compute at all. I want it built here, on terms we can inspect, with a library card at the front door.

I'm in. If you think I'm dead wrong about AI, data centres or anything else, you're exactly who I want at my table. Get in touch.

And yes, the problem child gets an invite.

Who's coming to dinner?
