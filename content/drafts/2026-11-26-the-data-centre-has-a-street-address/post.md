## A month after BC voted two ways at once

On Thursday I spent the day at SFU's Morris J. Wosk Centre for Dialogue on West Hastings, for the second Bridging the Political Divide dialogue. The building started life as a Toronto Dominion Bank branch in 1920. Now SFU calls it the only known facility in North America [purpose-built for convening dialogue](https://sfu.ca/dialogue/about/history.html). Good bones for this kind of day.

The timing was the point. On October 17, Vancouver elected [Pete Fry, the city's first Green mayor](https://vancouver.ca/news-calendar/official-2026-vancouver-election-results-oct-2026.aspx), alongside a progressive council. One week later the BC Conservatives took [64 of 93 seats](https://thehub.ca/2026/10/26/b-c-conservatives-win-majority-as-ebys-snap-election-gamble-ends-ndp-rule/) in Eby's snap election, and he stepped down as NDP leader.

Same province. Same month. Two very different signals. Everybody walked in carrying their own read on what just happened.

## What this thing is

Bridging the Political Divide is one half of [When the Map Is Useless](https://www.sfu.ca/dialogue/what-we-do/initiatives/map-is-useless.html), a multi-year SFU Centre for Dialogue initiative about how we make sense of things together when the old frameworks stop working. The idea is simple and hard: put people with real influence over public life at the same tables. Elected and former officials, journalists, union leaders, philanthropists, academics, community organizers. Then prove that disagreement can be rigorous without being dehumanizing.

The first dialogue, back in April, went big and abstract: what is democracy actually for? This one went concrete. Root causes of the divide, a debrief of both elections, and a deliberate push to bring in voices from across the spectrum.

It runs under the [Chatham House Rule](https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule). You can use what you heard. You can't say who said it, or who was there. So this post sticks to my own words, plus ideas that floated around the tables with no names attached. If you were there and want a shout, tell me and I'll add you.

## I never got angry. I got snide.

The day opened with an exercise. Think of the moment in a recent election that made you angriest, or happiest. Then dig for the value hiding underneath it. The theory: the values we list on paper are the ones we want people to see. The values that make our blood move are the ones actually running the show. Anger usually means one of them got stepped on.

My honest answer surprised me. I checked in and realized I never really got angry. I got snide.

I spent a lot of this fall at the data centre demos, helping host conversations, trying to build a container where people don't have to shout at each other through megaphones. And there was one guy who was always the loudest voice there. Always the most absolute position available. Megaphone in one hand, his photographer at his shoulder. By the third or fourth time I could finish his sentences for him. I caught myself cracking jokes to friends about our problem child.

It felt like he was stoking the fire to ride it into office. Maybe that's unfair. I can see why a young newcomer would grab that energy and point it at a ballot. But that's what I felt.

So what was the value underneath? Trust. Integrity. Respect for the slow, unglamorous work of building coalitions that don't eat themselves.

Not my proudest emotion. Useful though.

## The data centre has a street address

The thing that cooled me down came from Naomi Klein, who opened day two of [Futureproof Festival](https://futureproof.website/) this fall with a keynote on where to put pressure against techno-fascism. I bring her point up almost every day now.

Most of what scares people about technology has nowhere to protest. No office. No letter to write. No sidewalk to stand on with a sign. A data centre is different. It has a street address and a hydro hookup. You can stand in front of it, and you can call your MLA about it.

So people show up carrying everything at once. Loneliness. Rent. Big tech. Microsoft. The climate. And if you answer them with water usage stats, you've stopped listening.

Here's the kicker: a lot of the time the facts are on the builders' side locally. Plenty of protesters are fighting the American hyperscaler version, not what's actually being proposed here. Doesn't matter. Winning the argument about megawatts misses what they're actually telling you, which is this:

*I feel a lack of agency in my relationship to technology and power.*

Once you hear that, you've got a real framework for a conversation. You can sit down and talk about the stuff that matters instead of trading stats. (More on what public benefit from local data centres could look like in [this piece from last week](https://kriskrug.co/2026/11/18/vancouver-ai-data-centres-public-benefit/).)

## Both hands full

This is [the stance I keep coming back to](https://kriskrug.co/2026/01/24/both-hands-full/) with BC + AI. One hand curious and building. The other hand critical, demanding transparency, wanting to know what's actually being plugged into our grid. At minimum, peel back fifteen years of obfuscation so we know what we're getting into.

Some of my own crowd asks why I'd push to regulate the very thing we build with. That's why. If we want people to trust what we're making, we have to be the ones asking the hard questions first, out loud, on the record.

It also changed how I show up to protests. I can stand next to people I don't agree with on half their sign and still hear the part that's true.

## What else came up at the tables

No names, per the rule. These are the threads I'm still chewing on.

- **Values on paper vs values in decisions.** Every org has a values page in its strategic plan. Almost none let those values actually steer a decision. Somebody suggested every newly formed government should say which values will guide it, then show its work in public.
- **Gendered attacks are a strategy, not an accident.** When a candidate's worst childhood trauma becomes campaign material and a whole party lines up behind it, that's not a misstep. It's a decision.
- **Young people show up when politics shows up for them.** Housing, affordability, jobs, health care. Give them belonging and real roles, not just door-knocking shifts. And one election's youth spike is a moment, not a trend.
- **Organizing should be fun.** Food, music, art, kids welcome. People join for a cause. They stay for belonging and for feeling their contribution counts.
- **Facts alone don't move people.** On AI infrastructure and Canadian sovereignty, storytelling about local benefit matters as much as the numbers. And small founders can't just pick up and move to the US the way big companies threaten to. Family, immigration and roots keep people here.
- **Childcare as democracy.** Stories of $200 a day. A program built for everyone tends to survive elections better than one built for a narrow few.

## The homework

The organizers left us with an assignment. They call it the [Bridging the Political Divide Home Challenge](https://www.sfu.ca/dialogue/what-we-do/initiatives/map-is-useless.html): host up to six people at your place, including at least one person you disagree with. Then report back on how it went, and what got in the way.

They also have a podcast coming, so the ideas can travel outside these dialogues without breaking the rule. The next dialogue is set for March 22.

I'm in. If you think I'm wrong about AI, data centres or anything else, you're exactly who I want at my table. Hit reply.

Who's coming to dinner?
