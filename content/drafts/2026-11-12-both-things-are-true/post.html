Four minutes into my keynote at LaSalle College Vancouver in January, I quit circling and said the sentence the whole talk hangs on.

That's the spine of Both Hands Full, my signature keynote. I took it to LaSalle in January, to the World AI Film Festival Brasil in February, and to Vancouver AI in March. This post is the talk in writing, for anyone who wants to know what they're booking before they book it.

The left hand: name the theft

My work is in there. I put thousands of photos on Flickr under Creative Commons over two decades. Sharing was the deal. When I searched Have I Been Trained, I found roughly 1,800 of my images in a major training corpus.

A search tool shows evidence, not proof of which model used what. It still tells you exactly how casual the consent story is. Nobody asked me. They just took it.

The talk names five fears out loud and doesn't rush past any of them:

  • Stolen work. Humanity's creative output, scraped without permission, payment or credit.
  • The race to the bottom. Good-enough output is already undercutting what people get paid for craft.
  • The junior pipeline. The entry-level jobs that turned assistants into directors are disappearing.
  • Cognitive dependency. If you don't use a muscle, you lose it.
  • Commodification. Your style, turned into a preset somebody else rents for twenty bucks a month.

I teach AI for a living, and I don't have the junior pipeline solved. Nobody does. Anyone who says they've got it all figured out is selling something.

Don't drop any of it. That hand keeps you honest.

The right hand: say what's also true

I've rebuilt my creative practice around these tools, and I've never felt more capable. Not because they're magic. They fail constantly. It's because working with them forced me to get clear about which parts of the work are mine.

The right hand of the talk mostly belongs to other people: an animator, a VFX veteran, a writer, a book editor and more, from my cohorts and the BC + AI Film Club. Some of them walked in hostile and walked out building. Their stories live on the Both Hands Full stories page.

Here's what I'll say for myself:

  • Generation is cheap now. Taste is not. That's the whole argument of Your Taste Is Your Moat.
  • AI is a mirror. Bring vision and it gives you cinema. Bring nothing and it gives you content. More in Make Culture, Not Content.
  • Write for the bot. If your values aren't written down, to the machine they basically don't exist.

Don't drop this hand either. Not for purity. Not because the critique is easier to carry.

Two positions, not zero

People hear "both hands full" and assume I'm splitting the difference. I'm not.

Theft is wrong. The transformation is real. I'm not neutral. I'm holding both.

Most conversations about AI hand you two doors. The talk is about the third:

  • Embrace it uncritically. Become a booster, ignore the harms, and look back in ten years at what you helped along.
  • Resist completely. Become a doomer, keep your purity, and look back in ten years at how little say you had.
  • Walk forward with both hands full. Keep the critique and the curiosity. It's uncomfortable. It's also the only one of the three that gets you anywhere useful.

I don't reconcile the contradiction. I hold it. I made the same argument about data centres and the grid in Both Hands on the Power Cord. Same hands, different fight.

Stay in the room

The closing argument is the one I care about most. If all the thoughtful, ethical, critical people opt out, governance gets made by people who don't see the problems.

My first protester at a keynote sat in the back row with a camera rolling. She took the mic in Q&A and kept it. Good. She was right to be worried. If you're 20 and not pissed off right now, you're not paying attention.

Be angry. Just don't let anger be the only thing you hold. Get in the room, because the room is making decisions whether you're there or not.

What the talk does in a room

Both Hands Full is for filmmakers, artists, leaders and organizations trying to stay human while AI gets very good at making everything. It works best at festivals, creative conferences, leadership offsites, colleges and cultural organizations.

It runs in three acts:

  • The left hand. Naming what's true: the theft, the five fears, and the harms we keep softening into the word "bias."
  • The right hand. What's also true, told through real people's work.
  • The third path. Stay in the room, build with both hands, and answer one question: what do you refuse to outsource?

The São Paulo script runs 44 minutes. The LaSalle recording runs about 80, and the last half hour is Q&A that got honest fast: classroom assessment, VFX pipeline overwhelm, water.

And yes, there's homework. The Both/And exercise takes about 15 minutes. Write the critique. Write the curiosity. Then write the one dual statement you'd defend in a room of strangers. It's the first of twelve things to do after the talk.
