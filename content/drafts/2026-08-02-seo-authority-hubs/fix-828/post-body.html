Back in 2007 I linked this post to a ModelMayhem forum thread that listed the things models and photographers should negotiate before a shoot. That link is dead, and the Wayback Machine has no copy. The useful part should not disappear with it.

I have spent twenty years around cameras, models, stylists, assistants, clients, strange locations, good ideas, bad weather, and plans that changed five minutes after call time. The best shoots can feel loose and electric. The agreement underneath them cannot be fuzzy.

Most shoot disasters are not caused by a bad camera. They come from two people walking into the same room with different movies playing in their heads. One person thinks the photographs are a private portfolio experiment. The other thinks they are about to become an ad campaign. One person expects twelve finished images tomorrow. The other expects to choose three next month.

Talk first. Put the answers in writing. Then make the pictures.

Start with the job, not the gear

Write down who is involved, what you are making, where it will happen, and what success looks like. Name the photographer, model, client, producer, stylist, hair and makeup crew, and anyone else whose decisions affect the day. If the idea changes, update the agreement before the camera comes out.

A mood board is not consent. It is a useful visual reference. The actual boundaries still need words.

Usage rights, territory, and time

Be exact about usage rights. Is this for portfolios, social media, editorial publication, a commercial campaign, a gallery print, packaging, or all of the above? Name the territory too. Vancouver is not Canada, Canada is not worldwide, and an Instagram post is not a billboard.

Set the licence duration. Three months, one year, five years, or forever are radically different deals. Say whether the licence is exclusive or non-exclusive. Say whether the photographer and model can both use the work in their portfolios, and when they can start.

Do not hide third-party sale in vague language. If a client, agency, sponsor, publication, or stock library may receive the files, name that possibility. If stock sale is forbidden, write that down. Nobody should discover their face in somebody else's campaign because a sentence said "promotional use" and everyone pretended that meant the same thing.

The model release and its limits

A model release should describe the use you actually discussed. Its limits matter as much as its permissions. A release for a portrait series should not quietly become permission for political advertising, medical claims, adult products, facial-recognition training, or anything else that changes the meaning of the person's participation.

Spell out nudity and implied nudity before the shoot. Do not rely on a mood board, a wink, or "we will see how it feels." Define the boundary. Define who will be present. Define whether those images may be captured, selected, retouched, published, licensed, or sold. Consent can narrow or stop during the shoot. A signed form is not a trap door.

If the concept changes on set, pause and ask again. A better photograph is not worth turning trust into a technicality.

Paid, TFP, and what each side is contributing

State whether this is a paid shoot or TFP, meaning trade for photos. On a paid job, list the fee, deposit, payment date, overtime rate, expenses, and what happens if the client asks for more than the original brief.

TFP does not mean free. The model brings time, preparation, travel, wardrobe, performance, and the use of their likeness. The photographer brings planning, equipment, location work, shooting, selection, editing, delivery, storage, and the use of their craft. Write down what each person receives in return.

For trade, set the number of final images, the delivery format, the selection process, and the delivery date. "Some photos when I get to them" is not a deal. It is a future argument wearing a friendly hat.

Raw files, retouching, approval, and credit

Say who owns the raw files and whether anyone else receives them. My default is simple: raw files stay with the photographer unless the agreement says otherwise. If a client needs layered files, contact sheets, or an archive handoff, price and describe that separately.

Agree on retouching. How much skin work is acceptable? Can bodies be reshaped? Can scars, tattoos, piercings, wrinkles, or other identifying features be removed? Does the model get approval over sensitive selections, or is approval limited to a clearly named category of images? "Make me look good" is not a retouching brief.

Write the credit format exactly. Include the photographer, model, stylist, hair and makeup, designer, location, and anyone else the crew agreed to name. Decide where credit is required, what happens on platforms that strip captions, and whether tags are expected. Credit does not replace payment, but missing credit still tells you how much the collaboration was valued.

Time, travel, weather, and cancellation

Put the call time, expected wrap time, and overtime terms in the same place. Include the address, access instructions, parking, transit, accessibility needs, breaks, food, changing space, and a day-of contact number.

Decide who pays for travel and parking. If the location is remote, say who is driving, what happens if the vehicle fails, and when travel time becomes billable. If crossing a border is part of the plan, deal with permits and work eligibility before anyone packs a bag.

Write a cancellation policy. Cover illness, no-shows, late arrival, client changes, unsafe conditions, and weather. For outdoor work, choose the forecast checkpoint and the person who makes the call. "We will figure it out" usually means the person with the least power absorbs the cost.

Wardrobe, hair, makeup, escorts, and the physical day

Name who supplies wardrobe, hair, makeup, props, and location releases. List sizes and special requirements early. If the model is expected to arrive camera-ready, define what that means and reimburse the supplies or labour when the deal calls for it.

Set an escort policy before the shoot. A model may want a friend, parent, guardian, or representative present. If the location cannot accommodate that, explain why before booking. Never spring a closed-set rule on someone after they arrive. Minors require their guardian and the proper releases, full stop.

Plan the physical workload. The model should not be asked to carry heavy lighting stands, camera cases, or location gear just because there is an empty hand. If someone agrees to assist, make that a separate, explicit role. Provide water, breaks, privacy for changing, temperature protection, and a safe way home.

The two-minute check before the first frame

  • Who is here, and who is making decisions?
  • What are we making, and where may it appear?
  • What is outside the boundary?
  • Is this paid or TFP, and what does each person receive?
  • What are call time, wrap time, breaks, and overtime?
  • Who supplies wardrobe, hair, makeup, props, travel, and parking?
  • Who owns the raw files, who selects, who retouches, and who approves sensitive images?
  • What is the exact credit format?
  • What happens if weather, illness, cancellation, or a changed concept blows up the plan?
  • Does anyone need to ask one more question before we start?

This is a working checklist, not a substitute for a proper agreement. For major commercial use, minors, sensitive concepts, or serious money, use a contract or release that fits the laws where you work and have the right professional review it.

The point is not to turn a creative shoot into a hostage negotiation. The point is to clear the static so everybody can take risks inside boundaries they actually understand.
